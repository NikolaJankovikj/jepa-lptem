\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This thesis asked whether a self-supervised video model, never shown a microscope image,
learns the physics of nanoparticles in liquid-phase TEM video well enough to forecast it.
We built a simulator with an exact answer key, read physical observables off the latents
of a frozen V-JEPA~2 encoder and of forecasts made in its latent space, and judged every
result against criteria and predictions fixed before the result was computed.

The frozen encoder reads the present: particle size, total area and diffusion from a
single two-frame tubelet, at $R^2 \approx 0.9$ and at the MSD of a perfect detector,
although count stays below the bar. Forecasting is harder. Deterministic token-level
forecasts cannot decode to correct physics on this instrument, because half the
particles leave their token in one step and the optimal single guess erases them. A
position-free state, the frame average of the tokens, can be forecast without labels: a
linear forecaster on it carries each clip's growth beyond every trend-blind baseline, and
the remaining error in its growth exponents is what the noisy context allows. A
pixel-space forecaster trained on the same clips erases the particles, as the token-level
forecaster does, so forecasting in a latent space helped here only through the
position-free state it made available. Forecast
quality falls strongly with horizon and mildly with dose. Prediction error detects
particle merges as they happen but does not anticipate them, and simple baselines detect
them as well. Adapting the encoder with LoRA reads the present markedly better than
freezing it, most of all when labels are few, without changing these conclusions. None of
it transfers to published real LPTEM movies with a frozen encoder, so the results are
those of a simulation study.

The answer to the title question is therefore a qualified one. The model learns what is
predictable, growth, and not what is not, where each particle goes; and which is which is
set by the physics of the instrument rather than by the model. Along the way we found
three pitfalls that apply beyond this thesis: video encoders with temporal attention leak
the future into a latent forecaster's context; criteria should be set only after
measuring the floor or ceiling they imply; and published microscopy movies need to be
screened before they are used as data.
