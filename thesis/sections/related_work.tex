\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Machine Learning for LPTEM Video}

Machine learning for electron microscopy of nanoparticles has moved from detection and
segmentation towards the analysis of dynamics and, more recently, scientific
inference~\cite{toulkeridou2026review}. For LPTEM video specifically, most methods are
\emph{descriptive}: they measure what is in the frames. SAM-EM fine-tunes the Segment
Anything Model to segment and track particles in LPTEM video in real
time~\cite{samem}. SHINE uses self-supervised learning to denoise high-throughput
electron micrographs, an approach applicable to LPTEM~\cite{shine}. LEONARDO is a physics-informed
generative model of nanoparticle diffusion in LPTEM, trained on trajectories extracted
from video rather than on the video itself~\cite{leonardo}.

Fewer works \emph{predict}. Fu et al.\ train an LSTM to forecast future frames of in situ
environmental TEM video of Au nanoparticles under reaction conditions, in pixel
space~\cite{fu2022insitu}. The closest work to ours, Li et al.~\cite{li2026multiscale},
learns and forecasts the dynamics of nanoparticle self-assembly from LPTEM video, but on
hand-built coarse-grained fields: particles are first tracked, then binned into fields of
density and orientational order, and a sparse autoregressive operator is fitted to them.
There is no self-supervised representation and no learned latent space, and the forecasts
are not compared with a persistence baseline. We found no work that trains or applies a
self-supervised video model on raw LPTEM video, forecasts its latent state, and reads
physical observables such as size, growth exponent, MSD or merges off the forecasts.

\subsection{Self-Supervised Representations of Physical Systems}

Reading physical quantities from a frozen self-supervised encoder with a light probe is
an established way to ask what the encoder has learned. Qu et al.\ train JEPAs on
simulations of active matter, shear flow and convection, and regress the governing
parameters from frozen embeddings with attentive probes; the JEPA embeddings beat those of
a masked-autoencoder baseline~\cite{qu2026spatiotemporal}. Garrido et al.\ show that
V-JEPA, predicting in representation space, acquires intuitive physics from natural video
where pixel predictors do not~\cite{garrido2025intuitive}. Our Gate~1 follows this
protocol; unlike these works, we also apply the probes to \emph{forecast} latents.

\subsection{Latent World Models and Stochastic Prediction}

Latent world models such as V-JEPA~2-AC~\cite{assran2025vjepa2} and
DINO-WM~\cite{dinowm} forecast in the feature space of a frozen encoder and are judged by
how well they plan, not by whether their forecasts are correct. Several recent works ask
the second question. Gonzalez Laiz and Schneider define faithful latent world models by an
equivariance between the environment's transitions and the latent dynamics, and find that
none of the state-of-the-art models they study meets it~\cite{gonzalezlaiz2026limits}.
JEPA-x grounds latents in the physical state through cross-prediction and reports that
this makes the state more decodable without making it more forecastable~\cite{jepax}.
Scardecchia and Yang argue that the latent space of video world models should be shaped
for temporal predictability~\cite{scardecchia2026predictable}.

A second line addresses stochastic futures. A deterministic predictor returns one latent
successor even when several futures are plausible. Branch-JEPA replaces it with a weighted
finite set of successors~\cite{branchjepa}, Variational JEPA learns a predictive
distribution over future latents~\cite{vjepa_variational}, and Latent Particle World Models
learn object-centric stochastic dynamics from video~\cite{lpwm}. LeCun's original proposal
names two ways for a JEPA to handle uncertainty: a representation that is invariant to
what cannot be predicted, and a latent variable in the predictor~\cite{lecun2022path}.
These works were tested on driving, navigation and control, where actions drive most of
the change. Nanoparticles in liquid are stochastic without any agent, and their futures
are continuous rather than a few discrete branches, which makes LPTEM a demanding test
of both ideas.

\subsection{Simulation-Based Training and the Sim-to-Real Gap}

Training on simulated data with an exact answer key, and transferring to real data, is
common in microscopy, where real ground truth is scarce. The LPTEMsimulator of the Jamali
lab implements the image formation of LPTEM~\cite{lptemsim} and is the basis of our
renderer. The usual strategy is to randomise the
simulator's appearance widely enough that the real data falls inside it. Whether it does
is most often judged on image statistics. We judge it also in the feature space of the
encoder that the model actually sees, and find that the two can disagree
(Section~\ref{sec:res-real}).
