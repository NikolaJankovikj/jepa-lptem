\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Liquid-phase transmission electron microscopy (LPTEM) records video of nanoparticles
moving, growing and merging in liquid, at nanometre resolution. It is one of very few
ways to watch the processes that decide how catalysts, drug carriers and new materials
form, as they happen. The video is also among the hardest a computer-vision system can
face: the liquid scatters the electron beam, the dose must stay low because the beam
itself drives chemistry, and the result is a sequence of frames in which particles are
barely distinguishable from shot noise.

Machine learning for LPTEM has so far been mostly \emph{descriptive}. Recent systems
segment and track particles~\cite{samem}, denoise micrographs~\cite{shine}, or model
trajectories that were first extracted from the video~\cite{leonardo}. Methods that
\emph{predict} either forecast pixels~\cite{fu2022insitu}, spending capacity on
reproducing noise, or fit dynamics to hand-built fields computed from tracked
particles~\cite{li2026multiscale}. Meanwhile, self-supervised video models have learned
to predict the future of natural video in a learned representation space rather than in
pixels~\cite{assran2025vjepa2}, and such models show signs of intuitive physics
understanding~\cite{garrido2025intuitive}. Whether a model of this kind, trained on
internet video and never shown a microscope, has learned enough to forecast the physics
of a scientific instrument has not been tested. The test needs physics that is known
exactly, which real LPTEM video does not supply.

This thesis makes that test. We ask whether a self-supervised video model learns the
physics of nanoparticles in LPTEM video well enough to forecast it, and break the
question into three:
\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1.] Do latent states predicted by a forecaster on a frozen V-JEPA~2 encoder
    decode to correct future physics: particle sizes and their distribution, growth
    exponents, and diffusion?
  \item[RQ2.] Does the forecaster's prediction error rise before coalescence events, so
    that it anticipates them?
  \item[RQ3.] How does forecast quality degrade with the forecast horizon and with the
    signal-to-noise ratio of the video?
\end{description}
The contribution is an evaluation methodology rather than a new architecture. We build a
simulator of LPTEM video with an exact answer key, train probes that read physical
observables off V-JEPA~2 latents, and apply them to the latents a forecaster predicts,
comparing every forecast with trend-blind baselines and with the best that its input
allows. Every criterion and a prediction of its outcome were fixed before the
corresponding result was computed, so that either answer, a model that learned the
physics or one that did not, is a result.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A simulated LPTEM dataset of 2000 clips over five processes with exact positions,
    radii and merge times, rendered with a physical image-formation model and validated
    against its own answer key (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
  \item Evidence that a frozen V-JEPA~2 encoder reads particle size, area and diffusion
    from single frames at $R^2 \approx 0.9$ and at the MSD of a perfect detector, and that
    adapting it with LoRA reads them markedly better, most of all with few labels
    (Sections~\ref{sec:res-gate1} and~\ref{sec:res-encoder}).
  \item A structural explanation, measured on ground truth, of why deterministic
    token-level latent forecasts cannot decode to correct physics on this instrument:
    half the particles leave their token in one latent step (Section~\ref{sec:res-rq1}).
  \item A position-free forecaster that carries each clip's growth without labels,
    with its remaining exponent error traced to the information limit of the noisy
    context (Section~\ref{sec:res-rq1}).
  \item Answers to RQ2 and RQ3: prediction error detects merges but does not anticipate
    them, and forecast quality degrades strongly with horizon and mildly with dose
    (Sections~\ref{sec:res-rq2} and~\ref{sec:res-rq3}).
  \item A negative transfer result on published real LPTEM movies, together with three
    pitfalls that apply beyond this work: the look-ahead leak of video encoders into
    latent forecasters, criteria set without measuring their floor or ceiling, and
    published movies that are not usable as data (Sections~\ref{sec:res-real}
    and~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}
