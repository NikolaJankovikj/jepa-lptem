\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why Token-Level Latent Forecasts Collapse}

The token-level forecaster converged and beats both baselines in feature space (by 26\,\%
over persistence and 4\,\% over the context mean at $+1$), yet read through the probes it
falls below persistence within five steps. The two facts are consistent. In feature space
much of the L1 error is noise, and averaging the context alone removes most of it; the
context mean is close to what the loss rewards and is exactly the kind of latent the probes
cannot read (detection F1 0.25--0.33 at $+1$, against 0.58 for persistence). A lower
feature-space loss bought blur.

Token persistence (Table~\ref{tab:token-persistence}) gives the mechanism in numbers.
Half the particles leave their token in one step, so for most tokens the future is a
coin flip between particle and background, and the L1-optimal deterministic forecast is
background. This is the general weakness of a point-valued predictor under a branching future: trained
with a regression loss, it outputs a central value of the possible futures, which need
not be a possible future itself. Recent JEPA world models replace the single latent
successor with a set of branches~\cite{branchjepa} or a predictive distribution~\cite{vjepa_variational}
for this reason. Our
contribution is to show it on a scientific instrument, measure it, and connect it to a
physical quantity, the displacement of a Brownian particle over one latent step.

Why do V-JEPA~2-AC~\cite{assran2025vjepa2} and DINO-WM~\cite{dinowm} not run into
this? Their environments are close to deterministic, because actions drive the change,
and they never decode their forecasts: they rank candidate actions by latent distance to
a goal, where a blurred forecast is harmless. We copied their recipe (per-frame encoding,
L1, a short rollout) into a stochastic setting and then decoded the forecasts. The same
gap, between a latent that is \emph{decodable} and one that is \emph{forecastable}, has
since been reported for robotic control~\cite{jepax}.

The pixel-space ConvLSTM (Section~\ref{sec:res-rq1}) shows that the collapse is not a
property of the latent space. Forecasting pixels with a squared error, it erases the
particles within a few frames, the same way and for the same reason: a central value of
the possible futures of a randomly moving particle is no particle. The latent space did
not cause the failure, and it did not prevent it either. What separates the two
approaches here is not where the forecast is made but what is forecast. In pixels, the
position-free part of the state is not available as a separate quantity, so the
forecaster pays for the unpredictable positions with the particles themselves; the
frame-averaged latent separates the two, and its forecast keeps growth specific to the
processes that grow. This is a narrower claim than the usual one for latent
prediction~\cite{lecun2022path, assran2025vjepa2}, that it saves the capacity a pixel
model spends on noise. Our ConvLSTM did spend its capacity on noise: half of its
advantage over persistence is the noise it no longer reproduces, from the first frame
on. But that did not stop it from losing the particles.

A second finding is about evaluation. A frozen video encoder cannot be used as a neutral
instrument for reading another model's frames: V-JEPA~2 reads a 1\,px blur of the true
frames as particles 5.7\,px smaller, because its readings depend on the noise texture.
Comparisons between pixel and latent forecasters that read both through one encoder,
or that score pixel forecasts with features of a pretrained network, deserve a check of
this kind first.

\subsection{What a Forecastable State Looks Like}

Averaging the tokens of a frame throws away exactly what cannot be forecast, where the
particles are, and keeps what can, how large they are. In the terms of
LeCun~\cite{lecun2022path}, this is the first of the two ways a JEPA can handle
uncertainty: a representation that is invariant to the unpredictable. The caveat is that
we chose that invariance by hand rather than learning it. A ground-truth check makes the
choice less arbitrary than it looks: the fraction of particles that stay in a patch of
the image grows with the patch size, and only a patch the size of the whole crop holds
several particles at the density of our clips. At this density the frame mean is the
smallest region whose content is predictable. This agrees with the coarse-graining rule
of the closest LPTEM forecasting work~\cite{li2026multiscale}, which sizes its patches so that
each holds enough particles to define a field.

The remaining error in the growth forecasts is not the model's. Its exponent tracks the
ceiling of what the context reveals as the context grows, and neither a transformer nor
a mixture of predictors gets closer. The exponent bar was unreachable from eight noisy
frames by any forecaster. More context frames, or a less noisy reading of each, is the
only way to more accurate growth laws; within our dose range, more frames help more than
brighter ones (Section~\ref{sec:res-rq3}).

\subsection{What a Forecaster's Surprise Is Worth}

A natural hope for a latent world model is that its prediction error flags events. On
merges it does flag them, but it is not a privileged signal: a context average and a pixel brightness
change detect merges as well, and none of them anticipates a merge beyond the trivial cue
that two particles are already close. A deterministic forecaster facing random encounters
should behave exactly so: the merge is a surprise for one frame, after which the
forecaster has seen the merged particle and predicts it. Anticipating an encounter would
need the forecaster to represent where particles are going, which is what the
token-level analysis shows is not predictable.

\subsection{Simulation and Real Video}

Every positive result in this thesis holds in simulation only. No published real movie
falls inside the simulated feature distribution, and the forecaster fails on real input
from the first step. The simulator's appearance range, checked on image statistics, did
not cover real video once the comparison was made in the encoder's feature space with
the pipeline's actual preprocessing. An earlier check had found containment, but it was
made with the image processor's resize, which the pipeline later dropped, and was not
repeated. A premise has to be re-measured whenever the pipeline under it changes.

A second lesson concerns the data. Of 132 published supplementary movies, 122 had been
rescaled for display, so that their noise is correlated over several pixels and one pixel
is no longer one detector pixel. Such movies look like data but break the premises of any
method that reads noise or particle size in pixels. A test of the correlation of frame
differences at a lag of one or two pixels screens them out in seconds.

\subsection{Frozen Versus Adapted Encoders}

The frozen V-JEPA~2 encoder was the weakest of the three readers we compared, beaten
even by a small CNN trained from scratch. The information is in its features, since LoRA
starts from exactly those features, but not in a form a light probe reads well. Low-rank
adaptation recovers it, and is most valuable with few labels: with 10\,\% of the training
clips it matches the frozen encoder trained on all of them. When labelled clips are scarce, adapting a pretrained video encoder is therefore the better
choice than either freezing it or training a small network from scratch. The better
reading did not, by our pre-registered rule, change the growth-forecast conclusions.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Simulation only.} All positive results are on synthetic video, and they
    do not transfer to published real movies.
  \item \emph{Thickness.} The mean liquid thickness is fixed at 150\,nm, so the
    thickness half of RQ3 is not answered.
  \item \emph{Three criteria could not be passed by construction.} Gate~2's size rule
    (even the true future beats persistence by only 3--6\,\%), Gate~2$'$'s exponent bar
    (Section~\ref{sec:res-rq1}) and the first pixel baseline for RQ2, which could not
    tell particles from background (AUC 0.513). Each is reported as a protocol error;
    since then every criterion was preceded by a ground-truth check of its floor or
    ceiling.
  \item \emph{One pixel baseline, read by a different instrument.} The pixel-space
    comparison rests on a single deterministic ConvLSTM, read by a pixel estimate of
    radius while forecaster A is read by its latent readout; no single instrument could
    read both fairly. A stochastic pixel model, such as a video diffusion model, which
    samples one possible future instead of averaging them, was not tested.
  \item \emph{One seed.} Each model was trained once; the intervals cover the sampling of
    test clips, not training randomness.
  \item \emph{Different training protocols in the encoder comparison.} The frozen
    encoder's probes train for a fixed schedule, while the adapted encoder and the CNN
    keep their best validation checkpoint.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Closing the sim-to-real gap}: wider randomisation of the simulator's
    appearance, guided by the feature-space containment check, or self-supervised LoRA
    adaptation on real video.
  \item \emph{Stochastic forecasters for positions}: object-centric stochastic latent
    dynamics~\cite{lpwm} or forecasters that output a distribution, judged with a
    proper distributional score, instead of a single latent; and the same comparison in
    pixel space, against a stochastic video model.
  \item \emph{Learning the invariance}: training the encoder or a projection so that its
    state is forecastable, rather than choosing the frame mean by hand.
  \item \emph{Thickness and denser assemblies}: clips that vary the liquid thickness, and
    the dense assemblies where local fields, not only the frame mean, become
    predictable.
\end{itemize}
