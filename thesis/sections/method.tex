\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

The pipeline has three stages: a frozen video encoder turns frames into latent tokens,
a forecaster rolls the latents forward in time, and readout probes turn latents, present
or forecast, into physical numbers that are compared with the simulator's ground truth.
Only the forecaster and the probes are trained; the encoder never sees a label.
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the pipeline.

\subsection{Frozen Encoder and Feature Extraction}
\label{sec:encoder}

The encoder is V-JEPA~2 ViT-L~\cite{assran2025vjepa2} (300\,M parameters), pretrained
by masked latent prediction on natural video and used without any adaptation. It splits
its input into tubelets of 2 frames $\times$ $16 \times 16$\,px, so a $256 \times 256$
crop gives one latent frame of $16 \times 16 = 256$ tokens of dimension 1024 per two
video frames. The image processor's default resize (256 to 292\,px and back) is
switched off: it zooms the frame by 1.14 and shifts the ground truth by up to 18\,px,
more than one token.

\paragraph{Per-frame encoding.} Each two-frame tubelet is encoded on its own, and a
64-frame window becomes a sequence of 32 latent frames. This choice is forced by a
pitfall we found on the way. Our first feature cache encoded each 64-frame window in one
pass, as is usual for video encoders, so the encoder's attention let every latent frame
see the whole window, including its future. A forecaster given eight such context
frames is partly given the answer. Re-encoding the context alone agreed with the cached
latents at a token cosine of only 0.74--0.84, worst next to the forecast, and the one
clear win of the first forecaster (detection F1 0.80 against 0.56 for persistence at
$+1$) fell to 0.54 with honest contexts (Figure~\ref{fig:lookahead}). With per-frame encoding, frames shared between
windows agree at cosine 1.000. Every result in this thesis uses per-frame features; the
earlier results were discarded. The warning applies to any latent world model built on
a video encoder with temporal attention.

\subsection{Reading Physics off Latents}
\label{sec:gate1}

Two probes read a latent frame, following the attentive-probe protocol of V-JEPA:
\begin{itemize}
  \item an \emph{attentive probe}, in which a single learned query cross-attends over the
    256 tokens and a linear head outputs the particle count, the mean radius and the
    total particle area (z-scored with training statistics);
  \item a \emph{centroid probe}, a per-token linear head with a learned positional
    embedding that outputs, for each $16 \times 16$\,px cell, an occupancy logit and a
    sub-cell offset. Centroids are decoded at the local maxima of occupancy above a
    threshold chosen on validation data. The sub-cell offsets keep displacements
    smaller than one token, without which Brownian steps would be quantised to noise.
\end{itemize}
Both are trained jointly (mean-squared error on the observables, positively weighted
binary cross-entropy on occupancy, L2 on offsets; AdamW, learning rate $10^{-3}$, 50
epochs). Decoded centroids are linked into tracks with trackpy~\cite{trackpy}, and the
MSD over tracks, with drift removed, gives an estimate of $4Dt$.

\paragraph{Gate 1.} The encoder is taken to carry the present physics if, on the test
clips of the physics stratum, the $R^2$ of mean radius and of count are at least 0.8 and
the MSD-based estimate of $4Dt$ is within 20\,\% of the truth, read against the
ceiling of a perfect detector at the same frame rate and track lengths.

\subsection{Token-Level Forecasting}
\label{sec:token-forecast}

The token-level forecaster follows the recipe of V-JEPA~2-AC without actions. It is a
block-causal transformer over the $T \times 256$ tokens of $T$ latent frames: a token
attends to tokens in its own frame and in earlier frames only, and the output at frame
$t$ is the forecast of frame $t+1$. It works at width 512 (projected in and out of the
encoder's 1024), with depth 8, 8 heads, learned temporal and spatial embeddings and no
dropout, about 26\,M parameters. Its output is the current frame plus a learned update
whose head is initialised at zero, so the untrained model is exactly the persistence
baseline; much of the motion is a martingale that nothing can beat, and the model
should only learn what is predictable on top of it.

It is trained on 8 context latent frames with an L1 loss on per-channel z-scored
features, on the next frame (teacher forcing) and on a two-step rollout in which its own
forecast is fed back, with AdamW (learning rate $10^{-4}$, cosine schedule) for 50
epochs. Longer horizons are reached by autoregressive rollout. Forecasts are mapped back
to raw feature space and read by the frozen Gate~1 probes.

\paragraph{Gate 2.} Forecasts decode to correct physics if, at $+5$ on diffusion,
reaction and ripening, the size-distribution distance (Wasserstein) to the truth is at
least 30\,\% below that of persistence, and the growth exponent fitted to the probed mean
radius is within $\pm 0.1$ of the true one where it is defined.

\subsection{Position-Free Forecasting}
\label{sec:pooled}

Section~\ref{sec:res-rq1} shows that positions are not predictable on this instrument but
sizes are. The position-free state is therefore the average of the 256 tokens of a latent
frame, a single 1024-dimensional vector per frame. A ridge readout (regularisation chosen
on validation data) maps it to the z-scored count, mean radius and total area; it is
fitted once and frozen. It was chosen, before any forecast was made, as the first of
three candidate states (mean; mean and standard deviation; a $2 \times 2$ grid of means)
to read mean radius at $R^2 \geq 0.8$ and recover the true growth exponents within
$\pm 0.1$ from the true future latents.

Two forecasters are trained on this state without labels, each predicting horizons
$+1$ to $+24$ directly from 8 context frames:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A, linear}: the 8 context frames are projected onto the top 64 principal
    components, a ridge regression maps their coefficients to those of the future frames,
    and the result is projected back;
  \item \textbf{B, causal transformer}: each pooled frame is one token (width 256,
    depth 4, 4 heads, causal attention, a learned horizon embedding), trained with L1 on
    the z-scored state (AdamW, $10^{-3}$, 100 epochs), with the checkpoint chosen on
    validation L1 at $+24$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Gate 2$'$.} On diffusion and reaction clips, the reference is whichever
trend-blind baseline (persistence, context mean, pooled trend) has the lowest mean-radius
error at $+24$, which favours the baselines. A forecaster passes if (1) on every growing
process where the true future itself beats the reference, its paired gain over the
reference has an interval above 0; (2) its median growth exponent over $+1$ to $+24$ is
within $\pm 0.1$ of the true one on both growing processes; and (3) its median exponent on
static clips is within $\pm 0.1$ of 0, so that it does not invent growth.

\subsection{A Pixel-Space Baseline}
\label{sec:method-pixel}

To test whether forecasting in a latent space helps at all, we train a forecaster that
predicts pixels from the same clips, also without labels. It is a ConvLSTM next-frame
predictor~\cite{shi2015convlstm}: two stride-2 convolutions take the $256^2$ crop to
$64^2$ with 64 channels, two ConvLSTM layers of 64 channels ($3 \times 3$) carry the
state, and two transposed convolutions return to $256^2$. Its output is the last input
frame plus a learned update whose final layer starts at zero, so that, like the latent
forecasters, the untrained model is persistence. It reads 16 context frames (the 8 latent
frames of forecasters A and B) and rolls out 48 frames ($+1$ to $+24$ latent steps) on its
own outputs, trained with the per-pixel squared error that ConvLSTM-type
predictors~\cite{shi2015convlstm} and the LPTEM forecaster of Fu et
al.~\cite{fu2022insitu} use. Teacher forcing falls linearly from 1 to 0 over the first
half of 30 epochs (scheduled sampling), with AdamW at $10^{-3}$ and a cosine schedule, and
the checkpoint is chosen on the validation squared error of the full rollout.

\paragraph{Reading a pixel forecast.} The natural plan, reading the forecast frames
through V-JEPA~2 and the same readout as forecaster A, fails a premise check made before
training: a Gaussian blur of the true future frames by only 1\,px shifts the readout's
mean radius by $-5.7$\,px, and a held frame reads 2\,px away from its own latent. The
encoder's readings depend on the shot-noise texture, so it cannot fairly read the smooth
frames a squared-error forecaster produces. The comparison is therefore made on the
growth exponent, which is scale-free and has the same true value for both models, each
read by an instrument native to it: forecaster A by its readout, the ConvLSTM by a pixel
estimate of particle radius: the distance at which the mean radial intensity profile
around the true particle centres reaches half its depth, over blocks of 8 frames. On the true future frames this pixel reading recovers exponents of 0.39 / 0.51
against the true $1/3$ / $1/2$. A position-free alternative, the total darkness of a
frame relative to its background, which a blur cannot change, failed its own premise
check by 0.024 on reaction clips and is reported only.

\paragraph{Verdict.} For each test snippet, the exponent error $|\alpha - \alpha_{\text{true}}|$
of the ConvLSTM minus that of forecaster A, on diffusion and reaction clips. Latent beats
pixel if the median difference has an interval above 0 on both processes, pixel beats
latent if below 0 on both, and the two are tied otherwise.

\subsection{Adapting the Encoder}
\label{sec:method-encoder}

To measure what freezing the encoder costs (Section~\ref{sec:res-encoder}), we train
three encoders under the Gate~1 heads and loss, on the same input and split: the frozen
encoder (\textsf{F}, probes only, trained for the fixed Gate~1 schedule); the same encoder
with LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora} adapters of rank 16 on the query, key, value and output
projections of all 24 layers (\textsf{L}); and a four-block convolutional network with
stride 16 trained from scratch, which outputs the same $16 \times 16$ token grid
(\textsf{C}). \textsf{L} and \textsf{C} are trained end to end and keep the checkpoint
with the lowest validation loss. Each encoder is trained on 100, 30 and 10\,\% of the
training clips, as nested subsets identical across encoders, with the number of epochs
scaled by the inverse fraction so that every run sees the same number of samples.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Pre-registration.} For every experiment, the question, the methods, the
pass/fail criteria and a written prediction of the outcome were fixed in the project
plan, and committed to version control, before any number was computed. Results are
reported against those criteria even when a criterion later turned out to be badly
posed; such cases are labelled, and no verdict was changed after the fact. Analyses that
were not planned are labelled as post hoc.

\paragraph{Baselines.} Every forecast is compared with trend-blind baselines that use
the same context: \emph{persistence} (the last context frame held), the \emph{context
mean} (the average of the context frames held) and, for the position-free state, the
\emph{pooled trend} (the last context reading scaled by the median growth ratio of the
training clips at each horizon, one curve for all processes).

\paragraph{Floors and ceilings.} Before a criterion is judged, we measure what the best
possible input would achieve: the probes applied to the \emph{true} future latents (the
floor of any forecast read through them), the perfect-detector MSD, and, on the
simulator's ground truth alone, how much the context can reveal at the probes' reading
noise. A process whose floor cannot beat the reference is reported as unpowered, not as
a failure. These checks run on ground truth in seconds and decided, each time, whether a
GPU run was worth doing.

\paragraph{Statistics.} All intervals are 95\,\% bootstrap intervals over test clips
(2000 resamples, fixed seed), with the same resamples for every method, so differences
between methods are paired. Readout error is also reported as an sd-equivalent
(mean absolute error divided by 0.80, its ratio for a normal distribution), which lets
it be compared with the reading noise used in the ground-truth ceilings.
